Holyoke Veteran Archives — Biographical Narrative

The Last Officer of the A.E.F. to Fall

Rev. William Francis Davitt · First Lieutenant & Chaplain, 125th Infantry Regiment, 32nd Division, A.E.F.

December 8, 1886, Holyoke, Massachusetts  —  November 11, 1918, France

Who he was

Born in Holyoke and raised in Chicopee, William Francis Davitt played football at Holy Cross College — a star tackle for three years, and captain in his final season — before entering the priesthood. He was serving at St. Ann's in Lenox when the United States entered the First World War. Commissioned a First Lieutenant, he volunteered as a Knights of Columbus chaplain and was assigned to the 125th Infantry Regiment, 32nd Division, American Expeditionary Forces.

He was killed in action on November 11, 1918, roughly an hour before the Armistice took effect, and is remembered as the last American officer — and the last American chaplain — killed in the war. His remains were returned to Holyoke in 1921; he is memorialized on Chaplains Hill at Arlington National Cemetery, and the William F. Davitt Memorial Bridge in Chicopee bears his name.

The Vesle rescue and the Distinguished Service Cross

Chaplain Davitt was cited for bravery on August 6, 1918, during the operations along the Vesle River. Learning that forty wounded Americans were cut off in a ravine, he called for volunteers and led a rescuing party through a hail of machine-gun bullets. The party rescued every one of the wounded men, and not a member of the rescuing party had been hit.

The Distinguished Service Cross was awarded to the local chaplain for this brave and gallant action; he also received the French Croix de Guerre with palm. The clergyman who officiated at his grave was Rev. George S. L. Connor of Holyoke, senior chaplain with the 82nd Division — a college classmate and close friend, who had reunited with Davitt in France only weeks before he was killed.

“Last Officer in the A.E.F. Killed” — a contemporary account

He was the last chaplain to give up his life, and the last officer of the American Expeditionary Forces to die for democracy in the great war.

On the date of his death, November 11, 1918, he had returned to his old regiment after being attached to corps headquarters for a few weeks. He remarked that it was like a homecoming. He gave Colonel Eckert a flag that he had been carrying in his roll of bedding, proposing that it be used in the flag-raising at the close of hostilities. A few minutes later, the dispatch says, he started out across a clearing and a shell struck him. He was killed instantly, at about 9:45 o'clock that morning.

Chaplain Davitt was worshipped by the entire division. A casket of oak was made for him and lined with an olive-drab army blanket; another blanket was folded and used as a pillow. The flag he had given the colonel was draped over the casket. He was buried the next day with one of the most impressive ceremonies accorded anyone.

The cortege formed with two machine-gun carts, and an army mule — one of the chaplain's pets — drew the casket. The chaplain's own horse was led to the burying ground by an orderly, its stirrups reversed, while the regimental band trailed, playing the dead march.
